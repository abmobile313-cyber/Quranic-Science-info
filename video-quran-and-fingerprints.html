8 Billion People, Only You Have Your Fingerprint: Quran 75:4, the Honest Answer

What Surah Al-Qiyamah 75:3–4 really says about the fingertips (banān), what classical tafsir says, and what science shows about fingerprint uniqueness, explained honestly.

By Arsalan Ahmed Shaikh · Last updated 2 Nov 2026 · 3 min read

▶ Watch the video📄 Research PDF in 3 languagesAsk on WhatsApp

The verse

There are about eight billion people on Earth, and no two of them, not even identical twins, share the same fingerprints. Many videos connect this fact with Surah Al-Qiyamah, verse 4. Our own video did too, and some viewers rightly asked us to check our claims. This article gives the honest answer: what the verse says, what the classical scholars said, what science shows, and how far the link really goes.

The verse and its context

Surah Al-Qiyamah answers people who denied the resurrection and asked who could bring rotten bones back to life (see also 36:78–79). Allah replies: 'Does man think that We will not assemble his bones? Yes. We are able even to proportion his fingertips.' The key word is banān, which means the fingers or fingertips. The word itself does not mean 'fingerprint', and the verse does not talk about identifying people.

What the classical mufassireen said

Classical tafsir gives two main readings of nusawwiya banānah, and neither speaks of fingerprints. The first, narrated from Ibn 'Abbas and followed by 'Ikrimah, Qatadah and others, and recorded by al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir, is that Allah could have made the fingers joined like a hoof, so that a person could not grasp. Al-Qurtubi says this reading fits the flow of the verse best and attributes it to most of the exegetes. The second reading, reported by al-Qurtubi from Ibn Qutaybah and al-Zajjaj, is that Allah will restore even the smallest finger bones and join them perfectly.

So the 'fingerprint' reading is a modern reflection from the twentieth century. It is not found in classical tafsir, and the Quran did not teach fingerprint science. What the verse does do is point to the finest, most detailed part of the body to show Allah's complete power over the resurrection.

What science actually shows

  • Fingerprints form before birth: friction ridges develop on the fetal fingers around weeks 10 to 17 of pregnancy, and the pattern is then fixed for life.
  • Identical twins share DNA but not fingerprints, because ridge formation also depends on local conditions in the womb such as pressure, position and growth.
  • Small cuts heal back into the same ridge pattern because the template lies in the deeper skin; deep scars can change it.
  • Friction ridges are not only on the fingertips: they cover the palms, fingers and soles. Fingertips are simply where identification is most practical.

A short, honest history

Fingerprint knowledge came from human observation and science. Finger impressions were used on documents and seals in ancient China, and official papers in fourteenth-century Persia are reported to carry them. In 1858 William Herschel used handprints on contracts in Bengal. In 1880 Henry Faulds proposed using ridge detail to identify people in the journal Nature, and in 1892 Francis Galton made a statistical case for uniqueness. In 1897, in Kolkata, Qazi Azizul Haque and Hem Chandra Bose developed the classification method, with Haque supplying its mathematical basis; it became known as the Henry System and was adopted by Scotland Yard.

So what is the honest answer?

The objection that the Quran never uses the word 'fingerprint' is correct, and so is the point that fingerprint science came from scientists. The claim that nobody used fingerprints before the British is not accurate. And our earlier hook overstated the link; we have corrected our framing. What remains is still beautiful: of all the parts of the human body, the verse chooses the fingertips, the place where the Creator's fine detail is so precise that it can tell eight billion people apart. That is a reflection worth making, as long as we call it a reflection.
